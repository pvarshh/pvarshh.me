Currency

We rarely question why a dollar buys a coffee. We hand over paper, sometimes plastic, sometimes nothing but a tap of a phone, and walk away with something warm in our hands. Nobody stops to ask why a piece of cotton fabric holds so much power. We simply trust the system.

Information works the exact same way, only nobody printed a manual for it.

Every conversation is a transaction. Someone offers a fact, an opinion, a secret they probably shouldn't share, and in exchange they expect something back. Sometimes it's trust. Sometimes it's closeness. Sometimes it's simply the comfort of being heard. We rarely name this exchange for what it is, yet we participate in it constantly, from the moment we wake up to the second we fall asleep.

Few shows understand this economy better than The Mentalist.

For most of the show, Red John is the wealthiest man in the room, not in money, but in information. He knows who Jane is, what Jane fears, what Jane will do next before Jane does it. Jane, on the other hand, spends years working blind. He is chasing a name with no face attached to it, a signature with no signer behind it. Picture trying to paint something with no colors left on your palette. Or picture a puzzle where every single piece looks exactly the same. That was Jane's investigation for the better part of a decade. He wasn't short on effort or sharpness. He was short on information, and without it, even a brilliant mind can only guess in the dark.

That imbalance is what made Red John seem untouchable. Not because he possessed some innate genius Jane lacked, but because he held every card while Jane held almost none.

The tide only turns once Jane starts closing that gap. When he finally exposes the Blake Association, he isn't just cracking a conspiracy wide open. He is taking back, piece by piece, the advantage Red John had hoarded for years. Every name Jane learns, every secret he pries loose, chips away at the wall separating the two of them. As the information gap narrows, so does the gap in who looks like the more intelligent man. Jane doesn't wake up one day smarter than he used to be. He simply arrives at the same table Red John had been sitting at the entire time.

This is where the analogy sharpens.

We tend to treat intelligence like a fixed trait, something baked into a person from birth. But Jane and Red John's rivalry suggests something different. The intelligence we admired in that story was never about raw ability alone. It was about who held more of the picture. Red John looked untouchable only for as long as he had every piece of the puzzle and Jane had none. Once that gap closed, most of what made him seem so far ahead closed with it.
